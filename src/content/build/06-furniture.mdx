## Wood vs 8020

Most van builders go one of two routes: wood and plywood, or extruded aluminum. 8020 is the main brand name; compatible versions are cheaper and work fine. Some people also mix in IKEA components for bed spans — the Kallax shelving unit turns up in a lot of builds as a bed support because the spans are strong and the price is hard to argue with.

Plywood is completely valid. Well-engineered plywood furniture is strong, workable, and familiar. 8020 is more like Meccano for adults: cut it to length, bolt it together with industrial hardware, and you get something rigid without having to think about bending loads. In Canada we used FaZTech for our 8020-compatible extrusion — cheaper than the name brand, same profile. We used 15-series, which is 1.5" square stock.

People will tell you 8020 is dramatically lighter than wood. It is lighter, but not dramatically — not once you count all the brackets and hardware. Good plywood engineering is also fine.

## How we used it

8020 for everything at waist height and below: the bed frame, the galley, the bench seat. Above that, the upper cabinets are plain wood and plywood. There's no reason to use expensive aluminum above head height, and you certainly don't need your cabinet to survive a pull-up. Heavy stuff up high is bad for center of gravity anyway — so light frames, light contents, no regrets.

The head-height cabinets hold clothes, dry pasta, coffee, crackers. Not chocolate. That's not a joke — it gets warm up there in summer.

## The bed

Bed height is one of the more consequential calls in the whole build. You need to get into it without a production, and you need to sit up inside it without feeling like you're in a submarine. We got this right.

The walls of the bed area we got less right. Thin plywood, and it shows. People who properly frame out those walls end up with something more solid. We'd do that differently next time.

No windows back there at all — no rear door glass, nothing in the walls. Once the insulation's in, the sound deadening is genuinely good. Walmart parking lots, no problem. It's also a Faraday cage back there, so phones tend not to work. We've made peace with this. Honestly, we go to sleep faster.

The mattress is a 10-inch ND memory foam. More mattress than a van strictly needs. We sleep great.

## Kitchen and galley

The galley is built around the fridge, and the fridge ended up on the floor — the one thing we'd actually redo. Bending to the floor to get into a fridge loses its charm fast. Van 2 gets the fridge higher up, or gets a bigger fridge, or both. It holds about a week of food, which lines up with how long we'd want to be away from a grocery store anyway.

The pantry, drawers, and under-sink storage all work well. No complaints there.

## Bench seat

By the sliding door there's a low platform that hides the composting toilet, which slides out on a heavy-duty drawer. Low enough that it doesn't block the view through the door. A Lagoon table on a wall mount means one person can sit on the bench, one can swivel the passenger seat around, and you have a surprisingly decent place to eat dinner. The bench also turns out to be the step up into the bed, which is either clever integration or a happy accident — we'll say clever.

## Upper cabinets and the door cubby

Above the kitchen, a cabinet for kitchen supplies. Above the bed, cabinets for clothes.

Above the sliding door we built an angled open cubby instead of a full cabinet. The Transit's doorway doesn't reach the ceiling the way a Sprinter's does, so headroom at the entrance is already a bit tight. A full overhead cabinet there would make it worse. The cubby holds outdoor stuff — dog leash, headlamps, bear spray, bug spray — things you want on the way out the door.

## Cardboard aided design

Do this before you build anything. Make cardboard mockups of your furniture in the actual van. George from [Humble Road](https://humbleroad.tv/) does this on every build, and once you've tried it you'll understand why.

Sketches don't give you much. Even 3D CAD doesn't really show you what the space feels like. Cardboard in the real van does.

Our overhead bed cabinets ended up smaller than planned, and we think that was right. Clothes are a bit cramped. But we don't feel hemmed in back there, and that matters more than closet space. One person is always on the bed while the other is cooking — whoever's not at the stove is either up front in the swivel seat or sitting in the back. Being able to sit up, move around a little, not feel like the walls are closing in — that's what makes the van livable over months and years. We'd rather have cramped clothes than that.
