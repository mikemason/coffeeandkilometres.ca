## Start With What Matters Most

We designed the layout around the bed. Not around the kitchen, not around storage, not around maximizing garage space — the bed.

Here's why: the whole point of the van was to upgrade from tenting. And the specific problem with tenting, for me, is sleep. I'm a bad sleeper. A windy, cold, noise-amplifying tent was never going to give me a good night's sleep. So if we were going to do this, we were going to do it right, and "right" meant a proper bed.

Endy makes great mattresses — we have a king at home and it's excellent. Turns out they also make an RV Queen. Once we found that out, the layout decision became: build everything else around the RV Queen. Bed height was set to fit a 10" mattress (arguably overkill, but here we are) with enough headroom to sit up. Everything else followed from there.

## Designing for Three

We're two adults plus a labradoodle — no kids in the mix, which simplified things considerably. Plenty of families make van life work, but cramming more than two adults into a 148" wheelbase is a real constraint, and it wasn't a problem we needed to solve. This is firmly a two-person build.

That said, the dog adds a dimension that a lot of build guides skip. Dogs need a place to *be* when the humans are moving around inside the van — otherwise you're constantly stepping over each other and the dog is stressed.

Most fixed-bed builds drop straight from the foot of the bed platform to the floor — you stand beside the bed with your toes right at the edge, and the entire under-bed space is used as a rear-access "garage" through the barn doors. We pulled the front face of the bed platform back a couple of feet instead, creating a recessed alcove at foot level. That space is the dog's. It's partly enclosed — he gets a den-like experience — and it's surrounded by storage on the sides, so nothing is wasted. It's his spot. He knows it, he goes there when we're cooking or moving around, and he's out of the way without being banished.

This has been genuinely one of the best decisions we made.

(The tradeoff: bikes don't fit in the garage space. The bed platform sits too low. But we don't bike with the dog, so it wasn't actually a trade we had to make — it was just a constraint that didn't hurt us.)

## Galley on the Driver's Side

A lot of builds put the galley on the passenger/sliding door side — the logic being that you can cook with the door open and step out easily. We went driver's side instead, and we're glad we did.

The reason: sight lines. We wanted to walk into the van and see from front to back at eye level without a wall of cabinetry blocking the view. So we put a bench seat on the passenger/sliding door side — something you can see over — and kept the tall stuff on the driver side.

As you get in the van, you have uninterrupted sight lines from the front seats to the rear. No shower cabinet, no tall fridge (more on those choices later), nothing at eye level blocking the space. It makes the interior feel bigger and more like a room than a corridor. We think we traded some kitchen convenience for a more comfortable space to be in, and we'd make that trade again.

## Swivel Seats: Non-Negotiable

We treated swivel seats as a given, and if you're building a van for living in, you should too. They transform the front cab. Swivelled around, the two front seats become part of the interior — you get floor space back, the whole space opens up, and you can actually sit facing each other without perching on the edge of the bed.

It also makes the cab usable in bad weather. Instead of being trapped in the back when it's raining, you can spread out across the whole van.

## The Throwaway Build Strategy

We didn't jump straight to final materials. Instead, we built a rough version first — 2x4s, plywood, nothing precious — just enough to try the layout and start using the van.

This is highly recommended. We'd build a bit, go camping, learn something, come back and adjust. Some things don't iterate easily (layout is 4D Tetris — changing the bed position ripples through everything), but being able to *use* the van throughout the build process told us things we never would have figured out on paper. What's in the way when you're getting dressed. Where you actually put things down. Whether the dog's spot works.

The throwaway build also meant we were camping in a half-finished van instead of waiting until everything was done. That matters when you're trying to figure out if this whole lifestyle actually works for you before you've committed to it fully.
