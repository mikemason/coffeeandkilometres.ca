## Planning and sizing

Electrical is the thing that scares people most. It doesn't need to. We're not electricians — we just did a lot of research and built carefully. [FarOutRide's electrical guide](https://faroutride.com/electrical-system/) is the best place to start; they also sell an interactive PDF for system planning and wire sizing that's worth the money.

The part you can't undo: sizing. Once the walls are closed, adding capacity is a real hassle. Figure out what you actually need to run, then build for that number, not a rough approximation of it.

For us, the non-negotiable was induction cooking. No propane. That one decision drove everything else.

## Our setup

300 amp hours at 12 volts — 3.6 kWh of battery. Two 100W solar panels on the roof. And alternator charging through the Transit's upfitter connection point, which Ford built specifically for camper conversions; it can deliver up to 150 amps at 12 volts. We run a 60A DC-to-DC charger off it, around 750 watts, whenever the engine's on.

We drive every day, so the alternator is doing most of the work. The solar is more of a bonus than a strategy — fine when we're parked somewhere sunny, not something we rely on.

In real use: start at full charge, boil a kettle, cook breakfast on the induction cooktop, and we've spent about 15% of the bank. It works. The thing that would break this system is air conditioning — 3.6 kWh is not an AC budget. We don't have AC. That was a deliberate call.

## The approach question

Three ways to do this.

All-in-one power stations — Bluetti, Jackery, Goal Zero, EcoFlow. A box with a battery inside, you plug things in. EcoFlow in particular has built out their plug-and-play ecosystem enough that you can get alternator charging working without much custom wiring. Reasonable option, especially for a first build.

Fully custom component systems — buy a battery, solar charge controller, inverter/charger, DC-to-DC charger, wire it all yourself. This is the wiring diagram route. It's what we did.

[EcoFlow Power Kits](https://www.ecoflow.com/ca/ecoflow-power-kits/series) — two large boxes that handle most of what a custom system does, already integrated, expandable if you add capacity later. FarOutRide built fully custom systems for years before switching to these, which is the kind of endorsement that matters.

## Wiring it yourself

We used Renogy. The hardware works; we've had no failures. The monitoring app is genuinely bad — Victron's equivalent is much better, and if we were doing custom again with a bit more budget we'd probably go Victron. But the Renogy components themselves have been fine.

What caught us off guard was how much the supporting stuff costs. Batteries and main components are the obvious budget items. The marine-grade wire, fuses, breakers, bus bars, and connectors came to almost as much — maybe not quite, but close enough to be a shock. The van is in constant vibration, so you're using wire rated for it, terminated properly, protected at every connection point. Online calculators handle the wire sizing; the fusing you just have to do everywhere, because if something goes wrong you want the fuse to blow before anything inside a wall does.

Crimping turns out to be satisfying in a way that's hard to explain. You get into a rhythm with it.

## What we'd do on van 2

Probably EcoFlow Power Kits. The expandability is the main thing — build for now, add a battery later if you add AC, without redoing the whole system. The downside is that a failure could mean replacing a larger unit rather than one swappable component. We'd still take that trade.

And if van 2 has air conditioning, everything gets sized up from there. 3.6 kWh is right for induction cooking without climate control. It's not right for much more than that.
